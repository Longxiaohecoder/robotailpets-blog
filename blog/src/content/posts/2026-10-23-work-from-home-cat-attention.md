Imagine this: you are halfway through a video meeting when your cat steps between the keyboard and your coffee. You move a hand across their back while reading a message, then reach around them to unmute. Later, you realize you have been beside your cat all morning without quite choosing a moment to be together.

That hypothetical scene is a useful starting point, not a diagnosis. A cat beside a laptop does not tell us whether they want food, company, a different resting place, or anything else. It does raise a question about our own behavior: when we say we are available, what attention are we actually offering?

Working from home creates proximity. Attentive companionship still needs a place in the day. The framework proposed here is deliberately modest: make room for bounded interaction and undisturbed rest, rather than treating permanent, distracted availability as the goal.

## Attention needs an edge

A short interaction can still be intentional: you stop typing, notice what your cat is doing, offer an appropriate invitation, and follow through or accept their refusal. Compare that with repeatedly starting something while your attention remains inside an email.

The idea is simple. A recognizable beginning makes you choose whether you can participate; a recognizable ending makes it possible to return to work without leaving yourself mentally halfway through both activities.

Boundaries also protect quiet. A resting cat does not become an unfinished task just because you are nearby. There is room in a shared home for companionship that involves very little happening.

## Build around transitions you already have

Try placing a deliberate welcome before your opening work task. Put your phone down, look around, and give your cat the opportunity to approach. That might become a greeting, or it might simply be a quiet moment in the same room. In its guide to shy cats, Cats Protection advises maintaining routine, allowing the cat to approach, and sitting quietly without forcing attention. [Cats Protection: Shy cats](https://www.cats.org.uk/help-and-advice/cat-behaviour/shy-cats)

The suggested application is to borrow the restraint, not prescribe a response. Your opening transition is an invitation you can honor, not a performance your cat must join.

Before meetings, agree on boundaries with anyone sharing your workspace: which doors need to stay closed, which resting spot remains accessible, and who should avoid disturbing it. Choose a place your cat already uses if that fits the room. The point is to make your working arrangement understandable to the humans involved, without continually relocating the cat to suit the next call.

## Respond without explaining every interruption

The strongest counterargument to a planned interaction framework is that cats do not follow our calendars. Fair enough. A plan that makes you ignore everything outside its allotted slot would defeat its purpose.

Keep a separate, responsive play break available when work allows. First observe, then decide whether you can offer something and stay involved. If you cannot, acknowledge that limitation to yourself and return to the possibility at a workable transition. Every interruption does not need to become a game, and an interruption alone is not evidence of boredom.

Cats Protection describes play involving stalking, pouncing, chasing, and batting. Wand toys can imitate prey, with opportunities to catch them. Short games may be appropriate; toys can be varied. It also advises supervising toys that could entangle or be shredded, checking wear, and keeping fingers and toes out of play. [Cats Protection: Cats and play](https://www.cats.org.uk/help-and-advice/cat-behaviour/cats-and-play)

Our proposed workday application is to separate play from inbox management. Choose something you can supervise, close the message window, and let the activity have your attention. If your cat does not engage, you do not need to escalate into a more energetic show.

## Use a small decision framework

At a possible interaction point, ask yourself: Is my cat resting? Can I stop what I am doing? Is there an invitation I can offer without insisting? Can I follow it through?

If your cat is resting, leaving them undisturbed is a valid choice. If you cannot stop working, preserve the boundary. If you can participate, offer a suitable activity and respond to what actually happens. Adapt the questions to your household; they are not a timetable.

Finally, give finishing work its own ritual: close the laptop, put work materials aside, and become available for an invitation you can genuinely follow. Success need not mean constant access or dramatic play. It can mean small, finished moments of attention alongside ordinary quiet.

Before buying more toys, audit your workday. Look for the transitions where attention is possible, the boundaries that need clarity, and the rest that needs protecting.
